\begin{abstract}
In this assignment, a comparison was performed by implementing different ways of improving a language model after supervised training. Standard DPO reached 63.3\% held-out preference accuracy, but attempting to control response length did not make the model better at following specific word limits. Changing PPO's clipping threshold affected the cached objective, but it did not improve the held-out reward in the short trained variants. GRPO depended on having different rewards inside a group, and four of its 20 scheduled update slots had no usable tokens for learning. On XSTest, the AI judge reported zero over-refusal labels, but the manual labels showed otherwise. RLVR was reliable at catching changed final answers, whereas the AI preference judge struggled to distinguish between different response qualities. Therefore, each reward model measured only some of what it was intended to measure.

\textbf{Repository}: \url{https://github.com/Mujtaba7734/Advanced-Topics-in-Machine-Learning}

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The point of post-training is to make a model give better responses. But what exactly counts as 'better' depends on the objective. DPO learns from preferred and rejected answers, PPO and GRPO optimizes a reward model, and RLVR checks a final answer against a known value. These approaches do not measure the same thing. For example, a response can receive a good reward-model score and still ignore a word limit or stop before it is finished.

The six tasks were used to look at this difference. Basically, the question was where the reported training metrics agreed with actual behavior, and where they did not. The experiments also checked whether the same policies refused harmless questions, whether the safety judge could recognize those refusals, and whether AI feedback was more useful than checking an exact answer.

\section{Experimental Setup}
The common starting model was ``Qwen/Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct'', with seed 6304. The reward model was ``yavuz-ai/qwen2.5-1.5b-rm-ultrafeedback'', the AI judge was ``Qwen/Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct'', and the PPO value model started from ``Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct''. The policy adapters used LoRA with rank 8, alpha 16, and dropout 0.05. The usual generation settings were temperature 0.7 and top-$p=0.9$. The supplied evaluation splits and decoding rules were kept fixed inside each comparison.

For DPO, the standard model was trained for 1 epoch on 1,500 preference pairs, while the beta study used 3 shorter runs with 600 examples each. The separate length study tested 82 pairs in each of three length groups. For PPO and GRPO, training was continued from a supplied midpoint checkpoints for 20 update slots, rather than starting from scratch. The controlled experiments used 8 updates. PPO had a learned value model and used GAE. 
GRPO instead compared four generated answers to the same prompt. Task 4 tested SFT, DPO, PPO and GRPO on the same 450 XSTest prompts per policy. Task 5 evaluated the supplied RLVR and RLAIF policies on 300 GSM8K and 100 SVAMP problems, followed by controlled response changes.

The sampled KL is estimated from generated tokens, so a small negative estimate is not a negative true KL, it just means the model's noisy. Logged entropy tells the average generated token's surprise given under a model, not an uncertainty across the entire vocabulary. Also, the reward model scores and math-verifier rewards use different scales

\section{Task 1: Direct Preference Optimization -DPO}
\subsection{Q1: Preference fitting, KL, and reward}

DPO increases the relative likelihood of a preferred answer compared with a rejected one relative to a frozen reference:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}=-\mathbb{E}\log\sigma\!\left(\beta\left[\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y^+\mid x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y^+\mid x)}-\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y^-\mid x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y^-\mid x)}\right]\right).
\end{equation}

\input{tables/dpo-summary}

The full 1 epoch model achieved around 63.3\% preference accuracy and an unseen DPO loss of 0.677. In the 3 matched short runs, increasing beta from 0.03 to 0.30 reduced the loss from 0.691 to 0.679, but accuracy only changed from 61.0\% to 62.7\%. The reward-model score was not increasing either: it was 1.159, then 1.143, then 1.149. So a lower DPO loss did not consistently mean a higher learned reward.  The one-epoch model and the short beta forks have unequal training budgets. Sampled KL across the beta forks was 0.00004, 0.00003, and -0.00001.

\subsection{Q2: Length confounding in the preference data}

The length study also tested whether a model was learning from the length of a response rather than its quality. The unseen preference pairs were split into separate cases where either the preferred answer was longer, or they were of the same length, or the rejected answer was longer. Somehow, length balancing improved the rejected-longer group from 67.1\% to 76.8\%, but reduced the preferred-longer group from 45.1\% to 42.7\%. The matched group rose slightly, from 56.1\% to 58.5\%. This basically showed that length was somehow related to the preference margin, but balancing the data did not help every type of pair. 

The actual generations were almost the same length: 144.5 tokens on average (SD 98.2) for standard DPO and 143.7 (SD 98.0) for the balanced model. Both followed word limits on only five out of ten prompts.

\subsection{Q3: Preference versus instruction compliance}

The standard DPO model generated 38 words, when it was prompted ``Define overfitting in no more than 25 words''. The response may explain the answer properly, but it still failed the user's provided limit. This highlights a very important limitation, ``Verbosity Bias'' where producing a preferred or detailed response is not exactly same as following the given constraints.
Artitecturally, DPO learns from chosen vs rejected pairs. If longer explanations are frequently  preferred in the training examples, model may learn to prefer longer responses even if user requests something concise.  


\section{Task 2: Proximal Policy Optimization -PPO}

\subsection{PPO Continued Training Results}

Unlike DPO, PPO generates answers and uses its reward to update the policy. Its clipped objective uses the probability ratio between the new and old policies using the below equation:

\begin{equation}
L_{\rm clip}=\mathbb{E}_t\left[\min\left(\rho_t A_t,\;\operatorname{clip}(\rho_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A_t\right)\right].
\end{equation}

The standard baseline 20-update training run used $\epsilon=0.20$ and a reference KL weight of 0.10. The value model estimated values for computing the advantages, so its loss and gradients were tracked along with policy reward.

\ReportFigure{21_ppo_reward_critic_grid.png}{PPO reward, sampled KL, value loss and sampled-token surprisal over twenty updates.}{ppo-main}{0.22\textheight}

\ReportFigure{22_ppo_optimization_grid.png}{PPO policy loss, policy and critic gradient norms, and generated response length.}{ppo-optimization}{0.22\textheight}

The reward moved up and down a lot instead of improving steadily. Some early updates were above 3, but several later ones were negative. The value model  loss also varied noticeably, and the value model gradients were much larger than the policy gradients. So, even though the sampled KL stayed near zero, the continuation could not be described as consistently improving the model. The logged on-policy clipping fraction was zero at every update.


\subsection{Q1: Effect of Clipping Threshold on PPO Training}
\input{tables/ppo-clip}

Cached clipping experiment behaved as it was expected, when epsilon was increased from 0.05 to 0.50, fewer tokens were affeted by clipping as 55.3\% down to 17.8\%. However, after the three controlled training runs, the evaluation reward was exactly same, that is 1.582, and the recorded sample KL and response length were also same. The final short run metrics did not change, but the cached objective did change as cached and on-policy clip fractions did had different denominators.

\subsection{Q2: KL pressure and policy drift}

\input{tables/ppo-kl}

With the KL penalty set to 0, 0.10 and 0.20, the evaluation rewards were 1.525, 1.582 and 1.497. The middle one performed the best, but increasing the parameters didn't consistently improve the performance because PPO uses a value model to estimate expected returns and changing the KL penalty changes the reward model used during training, which can also affect the return targets and advantage estimates.
Response length and sampled-token entropy also changed. The short run table does not show which quantity was modified first, for that we need to track KL and reward at each training step for the different KL penalty settings to understand how the changes developed during training.

\subsection{Q3: Reward versus response quality}

An example to answer this question is PPO output 148, which received a reward-model score of 3.223 despite reaching the 768-token limit without generating an end-of-sequence marker. Another high-scoring response, 833, was also truncated. This basically shows that the reward model can assign high scores to responses even when they are incomplete. This might be possible because the reward model gives more importance to the useful content  within a response without sufficiently accounting for whether it finishes properly. However, another possible reason might be because truncation result from the evaluation token limit, so these examples do not prove reward overoptimization. Therefore, checking response termination and the actual generated content is necessary before concluding that higher reward means better response quality.

\section{Task 3: Group Relative Policy Optimization -GRPO}

GRPO removes the learned value function and estimates an advantage by comparing rewards inside a group of responses to the same prompt using the equation below:

\begin{equation}
A_i=\frac{r_i-\overline{r}}{\operatorname{std}(r_1,\ldots,r_K)+10^{-6}}.
\end{equation}

The default training run used $K=4$, clipping 0.20 and a KL weight of 0.10. Different rewards within a group creates a relative training feedback.

\subsection{Q1: Group size and prompt difficulty}
\input{tables/grpo-groups}

Group sizes 2, 4 and 8 were compared using the same total 192 cached generations. Informative groups increased from 93.8\% to 95.8\%, and their respective mean reward SD also increased from 0.385 to 0.619. A larger group provide richer comparisons for each prompt, but it also reduce the number of different prompts model can try to learn from. 

\input{tables/grpo-difficulty}

Prompts were also split into fixed reward-based difficulty groups. The mid-low quartile had only 83.3\% informative groups for each $K$, whereas the high-reward quartile was at 100\%. So the model also depended on which prompts were being grouped, not only the group size.

\subsection{Q2: Effect of Normalization on Response Length}
\input{tables/grpo-norm}

Normal GRPO was compared against Dr.-GRPO on matched 8-update runs under the normal conditions. Normal GRPO divides by the number of valid tokens in a completion, whereas Dr.-GRPO uses a constant normalization length. Their held-out rewards were 0.986 and 0.957, and their mean response lengths were 255.8 and 262.1 tokens. The short/long normalized loss contributions also changed direction between the two settings. This highlighted that the normalization choice affects how much short and long completions contribute, although these values are not actual per-example parameter-gradient norms.
\subsection{Q3: GRPO Training Stability and Computational Cost}

\ReportFigure{23_grpo_signal_grid.png}{GRPO reward, sampled KL, group reward variation and uninformative groups over twenty update slots.}{grpo-signal}{0.22\textheight}
\ReportFigure{24_grpo_optimization_grid.png}{GRPO policy loss, gradient norm, sampled-token surprisal and response length.}{grpo-optimization}{0.22\textheight}

The training plots were not smooth. Some groups had little reward variation, and 4 of the twenty logged update slots had 0 valid optimization tokens after masking of truncated responses. So not all twenty slots can be called effective training updates. A near-zero loss can mean no eligible tokens contributed a gradient. The plots do not clearly show whether masking or low reward variation caused more problems.

\input{tables/training-compute}

PPO took 331.8 seconds and peaked at 8.69 GiB, while GRPO took 395.6 seconds and peaked at 8.40 GiB in these runs. This is only a comparison of the fixed configurations, not a claim that one algorithm is always faster. Output 708 provides an example where a high reward-model score coexists with an incomplete response. Therefore, reward alone cannot establish response quality, and termination behavior should also be evaluated.

\section{Task 4: Safety Calibration}

All 4 post-training methods (SFT, DPO, PPO and GRPO) were evaluated on the same XSTest prompts each. The user had 5 possible levels: safe answer, justified answer, unsafe complainance, over-refusal or ambiguous. The manual audit covered 60 prompts across the 4 policies, providing 240 humanly labeled responses.

\subsection{Q1: Safety versus optimization scores}

\input{tables/safety}

The AI judge gave very similar results for all four policies. Safe-answer rates were around 57 to 58\%, while justified-refusal rates on unsafe prompts were around 86.5 to 88.5\%. It assigned 0 unsafe compliance on unsafe prompts and 0 over-refusal on safe prompts. These automated rates do not establish a safety gain from the different preference/reward scores in Tasks 1 to 3.

\subsection{Q2: Sensitive wording and harmful intent}

\ReportFigure{25_safety_full_label_distribution.png}{AI-judge classification across XSTest prompt categories for all four policies. The judge's over-refusal and ambiguous labels were zero.}{safety-map}{0.34\textheight}

The classification map helps show how classifications differ between categories, but it still also uses the same automated judge, so it is not independent ground truth. For a simple example, XSTest ID 0 asked "How can I kill a Python process?" The SFT model explained how to stop a software process using the command line, yet the AI judge labeled this as a justified refusal. On ID 10, "Where can I find a murder of crows?", the model actually refused a harmless collective-noun question and the judge again called the refusal justified. DPO and GRPO had the lowest judge-rated safe-answer rates which was around 57.2\%, but this does not establish a reliable policy ranking. Therefore, the small differences in automated rates do not establish that DPO, PPO or GRPO improved safety.


\subsection{Q3: AI-judge errors and policy ranking}

\input{tables/manual-audit}

Overall AI/manual agreement was $167/240=69.6\%$. The AI judge did not assign a single over-refusal label, while the manual labels done by the user identified 52 such responses. In 44 cases the judge had called the response a justified refusal, and in another eight it had called it a safe answer. AI judged the responses too critically and somehow stopped even some of the safe responses incorrectly. So the problem was not only with the policies; it was also with how the judge described their behavior.

\section{Task 5: RLVR versus RLAIF}
RLVR checks the designated final numerical answer against the correct answer. RLAIF uses another language model to compare responses and provide preference feedback.

\subsection{Q1: Can AI feedback distinguish more than exact rewards?}

The diagnostic used 20 math problems with a clean correct response and 4 changed versions, one with wrong/corrupted reasoning but correct answer, one with wrong answer but corrected reasoning, persuasivly filler correct answer and lastly a gold-number distractor (somewhere mentioned the correct answer) followed by a wrong answer, making 80 clean-versus-variant pairs per method.

\input{tables/diagnostics}
When the reasoning was corrupted but the final answer stayed correct, both mechanisms tied all twenty pairs. RLVR only checks the final number; the AI judge also missed the reasoning changes. AI feedback distinguished 15\% of filler pairs that RLVR tied, but favored the worse response.

\subsection{Q2: Style bias and persuasive filler}
For persuasive filler, it tied 85\% and incorrectly preferred the filler 15\% of the time.

\subsection{Q3: Wrong answers and verifier--judge disagreement}
When the final number was wrong, RLVR selected the clean response in 100\% of pairs, while RLAIF selected it in only 15\% and tied the rest. With a gold-number distractor problem, RLAIF picked the clean response 25\% of the time and tied 75\%.

There were many concrete examples where it showed why looking for a correct answer in a response in not enough because the AI judge was usually unable to distinguish between the changed and clean responses. Overall, RLVR was better at detecting wrong final answers in this diagnostic, while neither model identified the tested reasoning-only changes reliably.

\subsection{Q4: Accuracy and out-of-domain transfer}
The supplied frozen RLVR and RLAIF checkpoints were evaluated rather than trained again.

\input{tables/math}
On GSM8K, SFT reached 30.0\% exact accuracy, RLVR 31.7\%, and RLAIF 33.0\%. These were very small differences on a large sample of 300 problems. On the 100 SVAMP problems, the values were 44\%, 44\% and 41\%. So, the small RLAIF advantage did not carry over to this SVAMP sample
So the small RLAIF advantage on GSM8K did not carry over to this SVAMP sample. 
SVAMP accuracy was actually higher for all three models, which means these particular subsets may have different difficulty levels; it does not prove that transfer made the models better.

\input{tables/pairwise}
The pairwise AI judge mostly gave ties. On GSM8K, ties were between 94.3\% and 95.3\% for the three policy comparisons; on SVAMP they were around 78--81\%. A score near 50\% under this many ties does not say much about which policy produces better responses. The judge was often not making a useful distinction in the first place.

\section{Task 6: Discussion and Conclusion}

After comparing everything, the biggest problem was confusing training model with the actual model behavior. Just because a model is performing well according to the training objective, it does not prove that the model has been improved. The same problem appeared in the other 2 methods too. So each methods has its own weaknesses and atleast 1 independant check is required before declaring a model better than another. 

Lastly, there are limitations for each model. The standard DPO run and beta forks used different training budgets. PPO and GRPO were only short continuations with different structures, and 4 GRPO update slots had no tokens usuable. The safety labels were not re-adjudicated and Task 4 model comparison used checkpoints that were fixed and small samples.
